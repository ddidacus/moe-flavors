\begin{abstract}
Serving very large mixture-of-experts (MoE) language models requires tensor parallelism
or offloading expert weights to host memory, both constrained by interconnect bandwidth.
Prior work reduces this cost by encouraging the router to reuse a small set of experts per
sequence, via architectural changes such as option-critic temporal extension, or custom
auxiliary losses with a rank-matching regularizer. We instead treat working-set locality
as a reward and optimize it directly with GRPO, using an LRU cache simulator over the
router's own selections as a stateful reward -- no custom losses, extra modules, or
termination functions. We further combine this reward (CAR-MoE) with causal language
modeling on the same rollouts, regularizing distribution shift more effectively than a KL
penalty against a frozen reference, and introduce a prompt-conditioning variant letting
one policy target different working-set budgets by reading a literal instruction from its
own context. We evaluate on downstream task performance, working-set hit ratio, and
routing diversity, and find that CAR-MoE avoids the routing collapse that gives an
option-critic baseline its near-perfect but degenerate hit ratio.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Deploying very large mixture-of-experts (MoE) language models, such as DeepSeek-V3
\citep{liu2024deepseekv3} or Kimi K2 \citep{moonshot2025kimi}, requires distributing
hundreds of experts across accelerators, either through tensor parallelism or by
offloading expert weights to host memory and streaming them on demand
\citep{eliseev2023offloading}. Even with efficient serving systems such as vLLM
\citep{kwon2023vllm}, throughput degrades sharply once expert transfers become frequent,
since moving weights over a comparably slow interconnect starts to dominate compute. This
is worsened by the load-balancing objectives used at pretraining time, which explicitly
spread router selections uniformly over all experts, at the expense of the locality a
working set would need at inference time.

A natural fix is to fine-tune the router to concentrate its choices on a smaller working
set per sequence. One line of work draws on the option-critic framework
\citep{bacon2017optioncritic} and extends MoE routing temporally, holding an expert
selection as a macro-action until a learned termination function switches
\citep{shen2026temporal}; option-critic architectures are known to be prone to degenerate
solutions and require an explicit deliberation cost to avoid collapsing to one
\citep{harb2018waiting}, adding training instability on top of the extra termination and
policy-over-options modules. MELINOE \citep{raje2026melinoe} instead keeps the original
routing architecture and adds a cache simulation loss that penalizes expert switching,
paired with a rank matching loss against a frozen reference to prevent collapse.

We instead ask whether working-set locality can be optimized directly as a reward with an
established policy gradient algorithm, rather than a bespoke loss or architectural
change. We simulate a fixed-capacity LRU cache of experts at a single router layer and
use its hit ratio, or a soft relaxation, as the reward for GRPO/DAPO
\citep{shao2024deepseekmath,yu2025dapo}, whose group-relative advantage and trust region
already provide most of the stability a rank-matching term is meant to supply. We further
combine this reward with causal language modeling on the model's own ground-truth
completions, reinforcing two trajectories per prompt at once, and find this regularizes
distribution shift more effectively than a KL penalty or distillation-style reward. Last,
we introduce a prompt-conditioning variant letting one policy target different
working-set budgets by reading a literal instruction from its own prompt.

\section{Methodology}

\subsection{Stateful Working-Set Reward}
\label{sec:reward}

We simulate a fixed-capacity LRU cache of experts at a single transformer layer
$\ell$, chosen as the middle layer of the backbone. Let $p_t \in \Delta^{E}$ be the
router's distribution over $E$ experts at token $t$, and let $K$ be the number of
experts requested per token. We record a request set
$e_t = \mathrm{TopK}(p_t, K) \subset \{1, \dots, E\}$ at every position, including the
prompt, which is used only to warm the working set and is never rewarded. Let
$\mathcal{C}_t \subseteq \{1, \dots, E\}$, $|\mathcal{C}_t| \le c$, denote the set of
experts resident in the working set after token $t$, for a target capacity $c$. For each
$e \in e_t$, if $e \in \mathcal{C}_{t^-}$, the access is a hit and $e$ is promoted to
most-recently-used; otherwise it is a miss, $e$ is inserted, and the least-recently-used
expert is evicted once the capacity is exceeded. At training time, we route densely at
layer $\ell$, so that every expert participates in the forward pass weighted by $p_t$,
and use a soft reward, the probability mass already captured by the working set before it is
updated,
\begin{equation}
  r_t = \sum_{e \in \mathcal{C}_{t^-}} p_t[e], \qquad
  R(x, y) = \frac{1}{T} \sum_{t=1}^{T} r_t,
  \label{eq:softreward}
\end{equation}
where $T$ is the number of generated tokens and $R(x, y)$ is the sequence-level reward
handed to GRPO. This reward is stateful, since the value assigned to a token depends on
the entire working-set trajectory up to that point, but it requires no additional trainable
parameters and no assumption about which eviction policy the router should be tailored
to beyond the choice of $c$. We give the full derivation of the reward and its
relationship to the classical LRU stack algorithm in Appendix~\ref{app:stack}.

\subsection{Prompt Conditioning}
\label{sec:conditioning}

A single policy trained against a fixed capacity $c$ only ever learns one operating
point. We instead let $c$ vary per example and condition the policy on it through its own
prompt, so one fine-tuned model deploys at whichever budget is available. Given a prompt
pool $\mathcal{D}$ and candidate sizes $\mathcal{C}$, we construct, for every
$x \in \mathcal{D}$ and $c \in \mathcal{C}$, a conditioned prompt
$\tilde{x}_c = \texttt{[CACHE\_SIZE=}c\texttt{]} \oplus x$. The augmented set
$\mathcal{D}' = \{(\tilde{x}_c, c) : x \in \mathcal{D}, c \in \mathcal{C}\}$ triples the
pool and is shuffled so every mini-batch mixes budgets. The target capacity is recovered
at reward time by parsing the rollout's own prompt text, $c = \mathrm{parse}(\tilde{x}_c)$,
which makes $c$ a per-example
property of a training batch rather than a fixed hyperparameter, so the working set simulated
in Equation~\ref{eq:softreward} matches whichever budget the current example asks for.

\subsection{Combining Policy Gradients with Supervised Fine-Tuning}
\label{sec:sft}

GRPO estimates an advantage for a rollout $y_g$ by comparing its reward against the
mean and standard deviation of $G$ rollouts sampled from the same prompt,
$A_g = (R_g - \mathrm{mean}(R_{1:G})) / \mathrm{std}(R_{1:G})$, reinforcing whichever
rollout achieved the highest working-set reward relative to its group. We additionally
minimize the teacher-forced negative log-likelihood of the ground truth completion
$y^\star$ on the same prompt, so the total objective is
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal{L}(\theta) = -\mathbb{E}_{x, \{y_g\}} \left[ \frac{1}{G} \sum_{g=1}^{G}
    A_g \log \pi_\theta(y_g \mid x) \right] + \lambda_{\mathrm{sft}}\, \mathrm{NLL}(y^\star \mid x),
  \label{eq:combinedloss}
\end{equation}
with no KL term against a frozen reference. This reinforces two trajectories per prompt
at once -- the most working-set-friendly sampled rollout and the one known a priori to be
correct -- and when they disagree the supervised term keeps the policy anchored to a
fluent, correct distribution, which we find regularizes distribution shift more
effectively than a KL penalty or distillation-style reward (Appendix~\ref{app:sft}).

\subsection{Metrics}
\label{sec:metrics}

\paragraph{Working-set hit ratio.} At evaluation time we replace the soft reward with its
hard counterpart, using deterministic top-$K$ routing to match real deployment behavior.
The hit ratio of a rollout is
$h_t = \frac{1}{K} \sum_{e \in e_t} \mathbb{1}[e \in \mathcal{C}_{t^-}]$, averaged over
generated tokens.

\paragraph{Stack distance.} Since hit ratio is a function of capacity $c$, we also report
the LRU stack distance \citep{mattson1970evaluation}: an access at stack depth $d$ (an
unseen expert is a compulsory miss) is an LRU hit at capacity $c$ iff $d \le c$, so one
pass over a trace yields the hit ratio at every capacity at once. Appendix~\ref{app:stack}
connects this to the total number of expert transfers.

\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:setup}

We fine-tune two MoE backbones of different scale, Phi-tiny-MoE-instruct (32 layers, 16
experts, top-2) and OLMoE-1B-7B \citep{muennighoff2024olmoe} (16 layers, 64 experts,
top-8), using LoRA \citep{hu2021lora} adapters on the attention and expert projections.
Training data is the Nemotron Post-Training Dataset v2 \citep{nvidia2025nemotron},
filtered rather than truncated to fit the context budget. The working-set reward is
simulated at the middle layer of each backbone, with capacity fixed as a fraction of the
expert count and scaled accordingly across backbones. We train with GRPO/DAPO, group size
$8$, no KL penalty when the supervised term of Section~\ref{sec:sft} is enabled, and
compare five objectives per backbone: causal LM alone; the working-set reward alone; their
combination (Equation~\ref{eq:combinedloss}); prompt conditioning
(Section~\ref{sec:conditioning}); and, as baselines, an option-critic style router
\citep{shen2026temporal,bacon2017optioncritic} and MELINOE \citep{raje2026melinoe}.
Hyperparameters are in Appendix~\ref{app:hparams}.

\section{Experimental Results}
\label{sec:results}

We report downstream task performance (MMLU \citep{hendrycks2021mmlu}, MMMLU, GSM8K
\citep{cobbe2021gsm8k}, HumanEval \citep{chen2021humaneval}, and MATH
\citep{hendrycks2021math}), working-set efficiency (hit ratio, stack distance, throughput), and an
interpretability analysis of expert specialization, comparing every objective of
Section~\ref{sec:setup} on both backbones. Results are organized by whether the policy
is conditioned on a target working-set size at inference time.

\paragraph{Note on LoRA.} Every objective, baselines included, is a LoRA adapter over the
frozen backbone, so scores are expected somewhat lower than a full fine-tune, equally for
every method. We also do not merge the adapter before timing throughput, so we control for
its own inference-time cost by timing the base model's compute pass plus each objective's
own working-set miss count, rather than its adapter-inflated decode time
(Appendix~\ref{app:fulltables}).

Phi-tiny-MoE-instruct was pretrained with 16 experts and top-2 routing, while OLMoE-1B-7B
was pretrained with 64 experts and top-8 routing, a much larger pool and wider fan-out
that matters for the collapse discussed below. On both backbones CAR-MoE is competitive
with or better than the base model on most tasks, and combining it with the supervised
loss (Phi-tiny-MoE only, so far) raises the hit ratio further still to 0.781 while also
recovering the reward-only variant's throughput cost (Appendix~\ref{app:fulltables}) --
option-critic and MELINOE instead lose several points of accuracy, most sharply on MATH,
GSM8K, and HumanEval, for a comparable or larger hit-ratio gain. On Phi-tiny-MoE, the
option-critic controller sharply peaks the routing distribution (ECI falls from 0.89 to
0.28) while reaching a near-perfect hit ratio, which we interpret as routing collapsing
onto a handful of experts that stay resident for the entire sequence rather than
genuinely tracking locality, at considerable cost to downstream accuracy; CAR-MoE~(+SFT)
only drops ECI to 0.71. With OLMoE-1B-7B, this collapse does not happen, arguably because
of the larger model and its wider top-8 routing: every variant keeps a near-uniform
distribution (ECI $\ge 0.84$), and CAR-MoE is the one that keeps a competitive hit ratio

\subsection{With Prompt Conditioning}
\label{sec:results-conditioned}

The prompt-conditioned policy is trained once per backbone but evaluated at every working-set
capacity it was conditioned on, by prefixing each prompt with the target capacity
(Section~\ref{sec:conditioning}). Table~\ref{tab:conditioned} in Appendix~\ref{app:fulltables}
reports both backbones swept over every capacity they were trained on (Phi-tiny-MoE:
$c\in\{2,4,8\}$, single seed; OLMoE: $c\in\{8,16,32\}$, averaged over two seeds), with
capacity expressed as a \% of each backbone's total expert count so the two sweeps share
one axis. As expected, hit ratio improves monotonically with capacity on both backbones,
from 0.51-0.65 at the smallest capacity to 0.90-0.94 at the largest; tokens/second also
increases with capacity, since a larger working set both raises the hit ratio and is
evaluated with a fixed generation length, so the throughput gain compounds rather than
trading off against the hit-ratio gain as it does for the fixed-capacity CAR-MoE of
Table~\ref{tab:mainresults}. A single prompt-conditioned policy therefore lets a
deployment pick a hit-ratio/throughput operating point at inference time without
retraining, unlike the fixed-capacity variants of Section~\ref{sec:results-unconditioned}.

For reference, OLMoE's prompt-conditioned checkpoint evaluated unconditioned on the
downstream task suite scores MMLU 0.544, MMMLU 0.374, GSM8K 0.662, HumanEval 0.268, and
MATH 0.053 -- in the same range as Table~\ref{tab:mainresults}'s unconditioned objectives,
so the conditioning prefix does not measurably harm general task performance.
Figures~\ref{fig:scaling_hit}--\ref{fig:scaling_tps} in Appendix~\ref{app:figures} plot
Table~\ref{tab:conditioned} against capacity, and the same appendix's routing traces
reveal an \emph{expert chunking} pattern under CAR-MoE -- contiguous runs of tokens
sharing an expert, resembling the option-critic's macro-actions but emerging from a
stateless per-token reward rather than an architectural hold.

\section{Conclusion}

We recast working-set-friendly MoE fine-tuning as a policy gradient problem, using an LRU
cache simulator as a stateful reward (CAR-MoE) in place of a custom loss or architectural
change. Combining it with causal LM on the same rollouts regularizes distribution shift
more effectively than a KL penalty, and prompt conditioning lets a single policy serve
multiple working-set budgets. On Phi-tiny-MoE, CAR-MoE also avoids the routing collapse
that gives the option-critic baseline its near-perfect but degenerate hit ratio, and its
routing traces show a "chunking" pattern that emerges without any explicit macro-action
machinery.

\section{Stack Distance and the Number of Expert Transfers}
\label{app:stack}

We make precise the relationship between the stack distance defined in
Section~\ref{sec:metrics} and the total number of CPU-GPU expert transfers an offloaded
serving system would incur. Let $r^{(\ell,t)} \in \{0,1\}^E$ denote the binary request
vector at layer $\ell$ and token $t$, with $\|r^{(\ell,t)}\|_1 = K$, and let
$\mathcal{C}^{(\ell,t)}$ be the set of experts resident in the working set after token $t$
under an LRU eviction policy of capacity $c$. The number of working-set misses, which equals
the number of transfers under a cold-start-free serving system, is
\begin{equation}
  N_{\mathrm{miss}}(\ell, c) = \sum_{t=1}^{T} \sum_{e \in e_t} \mathbb{1}\!\left[
    e \notin \mathcal{C}^{(\ell,t^-)}\right] = \sum_{t=1}^{T} \sum_{e \in e_t}
    \mathbb{1}[d(e, t) > c],
\end{equation}
where $d(e, t)$ is the stack distance of the access to $e$ at token $t$, treating a
compulsory miss as $d = \infty$. Since $d(e, t)$ does not depend on $c$, computing it
once for every access yields $N_{\mathrm{miss}}(\ell, c)$ for every candidate capacity
$c$ in a single pass over the routing trace, which is how we obtain the hit ratio curves
reported in Section~\ref{sec:results} without re-simulating the working set separately per
capacity.

\section{Regularizing Distribution Shift Without a KL Penalty}
\label{app:sft}

A KL penalty against a frozen reference, or a distillation-style reward that scores a
rollout by the reference's own log-likelihood of it, both anchor the policy to the
reference's original behavior, but neither one ever increases the likelihood the policy
assigns to a completion known to be correct, since both are only ever satisfied by
staying close to what the reference already does. The supervised term in
Equation~\ref{eq:combinedloss} instead directly minimizes the negative log-likelihood of
the ground truth completion under the current policy, which pushes that likelihood up
regardless of what the reference would have assigned it, while the working-set reward
simultaneously pushes the policy toward whichever sampled rollout was most working-set
friendly. In our runs, we find the KL penalty to be redundant once the supervised term
is enabled, and disable it accordingly, relying on the supervised term alone to keep the
policy from drifting into working-set-friendly but degenerate completions.

\section{Expert Routing Traces and Prompt-Conditioned Scaling}
\label{app:figures}

Figures~\ref{fig:phi_base}--\ref{fig:scaling_tps} collect every routing-trace and
scaling figure generated for the results of Section~\ref{sec:results}, for selection into
the main text. Each expert-routing-trace figure shows, for 16 held-out prompts (rows)
generated on-policy by the given (backbone, objective) pair, the top-1 expert chosen at
each of three quartile-spaced layers of the backbone (25\%/50\%/75\% depth) over every
generated token (columns), colored by expert id.

\paragraph{Expert chunking.} Under CAR-MoE (Figures~\ref{fig:phi_reward},
\ref{fig:olmoe_reward}), the router's per-token expert choice visibly organizes into
contiguous runs of tokens assigned to the same expert -- segments of consecutive
positions sharing an expert id -- rather than the token-by-token switching visible in the
base model's trace (Figures~\ref{fig:phi_base}, \ref{fig:olmoe_base}). We call this
pattern \emph{expert chunking}: it resembles the macro-actions an option-critic policy
holds explicitly until a learned termination function fires
(Figures~\ref{fig:phi_controller}, \ref{fig:olmoe_controller}), except here it emerges
from a stateless per-token reward rather than being architecturally hard-coded. This
suggests the working-set hit ratio itself acts as a training pressure that pushes an
ordinary per-token router toward temporally chunked expert use without introducing an
explicit option, termination function, or macro-action -- the router discovers a soft
form of the option-critic's holding behavior as a side effect of being rewarded for
reuse, rather than by construction.
